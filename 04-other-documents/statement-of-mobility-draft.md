## Statement of mobility

Erol Orel, Ambizione 2026. Host: Institute of Global Health, Faculty of Medicine, University of
Geneva.

### 1. Institutional academic mobility

I studied economics and econometrics at the University of Geneva, worked for eleven years in
finance, and came back to the University in 2019 for a doctorate in biomedical sciences at the
Institute of Global Health, which I completed in December 2023. I have stayed at the Institute
since then as a senior research associate. My doctoral advisor, Prof. Olivia Keiser, is the
contact person for this application, so the host is the institution where I obtained my doctorate
and where I have worked for more than a year.

I chose it because the project is built on Geneva. The outcome data come from the emergency call
centre (144), the emergency department and the hospital of the HUG, the legionellosis data come
from the cantonal services, and the evidence-extraction prototype was developed here. Over the
last two years my work has moved across units. I collaborate with the Data Science for Digital
Health group of the Department of Radiology and Medical Informatics on text mining, with the HUG
emergency service and the 144 call centre on operational data and a joint systematic review, and
with the cantonal services on the legionellosis study, for which I carry out the full analysis.
What Ambizione changes is my position. I would lead my own programme, with my own question,
budget and team, alongside the Institute's groups rather than inside one. The host confirmation,
signed by the contact person and the director of the Institute, says so.

### 2. Transnational academic mobility

My research has been international in its subjects and partners more than in my address. During
the doctorate I worked on HIV prediction across East and Southern Africa and, as senior
statistician, I mentored the analysis of a seroprevalence study in Cameroon published in Nature
Communications. During the pandemic I helped build the COVID-19 epidemiological dashboard of the
WHO African Region. I presented this work abroad, at the CODATA and DDI Alliance workshop in
Dagstuhl, Germany, in 2019 and at the International Workshop on HIV Observational Databases in
Greece in 2023. Since 2024 I am part of the Franco-Swiss Interreg consortium GESICA on emergency
and crisis management, with the HUG as Swiss lead, the CHUV, the University of Neuchâtel, the
HEIG-VD and Techwan, and on the French side the Université Marie et Louis Pasteur and the HES-SO.
I attended its scientific seminar in Besançon on 22 June 2026. Since 2026 I contribute to the
Horizon Europe project GeoAI4EI on epidemic intelligence, whose kick-off meeting I attended in
Münster, Germany, on 26 to 28 August 2026.

Under the grant these two consortia continue, with their regular cross-border meetings. I plan
two short stays, of one to two weeks, with groups abroad working on forecast evaluation and
nowcasting, to be arranged once the first results exist. I intend to present the project at
conferences in its field, among them ESCAIDE, Epidemics, the International Symposium on
Forecasting, the ISBA World Meeting and the ICASR meeting on the automation of systematic
reviews. I will also host visiting researchers in Geneva, since the benchmark, the code and the
evaluation set-up of the project are open and meant to be reused.

### 3. Intersectoral mobility

Between 2007 and 2018 I worked in asset management in Geneva, first as a risk manager and
quantitative analyst, then as cofounder of a small firm. The work was about rare and costly
events, with extreme-value methods for risk measures, stress tests and scenario analysis, and it
included setting up investment vehicles in Luxembourg. I obtained the Financial Risk Manager
certification in 2018. In 2019 I left that sector for biomedical research. That move is the
origin of this project's way of thinking, which treats a health crisis as a rare event whose cost
depends on a decision taken early, and of its methods for peaks and thresholds.

The bridge has since run in the other direction. LiteRev was funded by CHF 70,000 obtained with
my group from the University, the Fondation Ernst et Lucie Schmidheiny, Venture Kick and Mimosa,
funders from the innovation sector, and I followed the I-Teams medical innovation programme in
2023. Under the grant, the fourth work package is done with the services themselves. The costs
of wrong decisions are elicited from two to five experienced people in the emergency services,
and the results go back to the partners who provided the data.

### 4. Interdisciplinary mobility

My training is in econometrics. I then worked in quantitative finance, moved to epidemiology and
public health for the doctorate, and built tools for evidence synthesis that draw on natural
language processing and machine learning. Each step is visible in my publications, from HIV
prediction with gradient-boosted models to the LiteRev evaluation in JMIR, the Delta and Omicron
severity comparison in a Swiss hospital, and a machine-learning classification across WHO STEPS
surveys that I supervised as last author.

HEADSTART brings these fields together around one question. Work package 1 is text mining and
measurement error, work packages 2 and 3 are Bayesian time-series modelling and forecast
evaluation, and work package 4 is decision analysis with emergency physicians and dispatchers.
Running the four together, with the same data and the same registered rules, is the
interdisciplinary step of the project.

### 5. Intellectual mobility

Since 2024 I teach statistics and epidemiology on the MAS in Public Health to practising
clinicians and public-health professionals. They use methods rather than publish about them, and
teaching them has shaped the way this project judges a forecast, by the decision it changes
rather than by a score alone. I have supervised eight MAS theses and mentored about six junior
researchers at the Institute. LiteRev is used by other teams, including in the systematic review
of artificial intelligence in emergency medical services led by the HUG within GESICA.

Under the grant, the outputs are meant to be used outside academia. The extraction benchmark and
the code are open. The thresholds and costs elicited from the emergency services are documented
and returned to them. For the demand tests that pass validation and that the services authorise,
forecasts run on a daily dashboard in observation mode. Results, positive or negative, are
reported to the cantonal partners and summarised for a general audience on the platform. A
negative result matters as much here, because it tells services where published evidence should
not guide an operational decision.
