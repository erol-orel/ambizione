# HEADSTART, summary, version of 21 October 2026 (evening)

Every winter, respiratory infections such as influenza, RSV and COVID-19 increase emergency calls or hospital admissions in Geneva, as everywhere else. In summer a heat wave does the same within days. The people who run those services have to decide early whether to bring in extra staff or open additional beds, and the earliest decisions are the ones that count most. Ideally, a forecast would tell them whether a wave is coming, how fast it will rise and how high. The weather, the air quality, the school holidays, the virus load in the wastewater and the routine surveillance figures are there to build it on. Past seasons give a first idea of what these figures mean for the services, but no two episodes are alike. The virus may be a new variant, the heat may come earlier or last longer, the population may be more or less immune, people may behave differently, and the services may have changed their beds, their staff or the way they record their cases, often several of these at once. So, how the figures will turn into demand has to be learnt again for each episode, and at its start there are not yet enough days of local calls or admissions to learn it from. To forecast anyway, a model has to start from knowledge that does not come from the local data, such as which of these figures to watch, how quickly a change in them turns into more calls or admissions, how long patients stay in hospital, and what level of demand counts as serious.

Much of this has been measured before, in other places or in past seasons, and the results are in published studies. One study has counted how many more people come to an emergency department for each extra degree on a hot day. Another has measured how many days pass between a rise in emergency calls and a rise in admissions. A heat plan states the temperature at which it sends a warning. A review of past epidemics says which models forecast best in the first weeks. A hospital has reported how long its COVID-19 patients stayed and what share needed intensive care. What was measured elsewhere may not fit Geneva exactly, but before an episode and in its first days it is the best starting point there is, and the local data can correct it as they come in. Bringing such results together is what a systematic review does. It means searching the literature, screening hundreds or thousands of papers, reading the relevant ones and extracting the information by hand. A good review is the most reliable summary of what is known, but it takes months, so it is rarely done for forecasting, and it is out of date by the time a crisis comes.

A forecast cannot wait for that. If this knowledge could be gathered quickly and accurately, and kept up to date, it could feed a forecast that runs continuously, for each kind of crisis, combining what the studies say with the weather, the wastewater, the surveillance figures and the first local calls or admissions. When a heat wave is announced or an epidemic appears in the wastewater or in neighbouring regions, the services would see it rising, how fast and how high, while there is still time to prepare, instead of waiting for their own numbers to accumulate.

Software can now do a large part of the review work. With colleagues, I developed LiteRev, a tool that searches scientific papers and ranks them by how relevant they are to a given question, so that researchers find the studies they need without reading so many. I have since extended it so that it also extracts from each study the kind of information described above and updates it as new studies are published. This extension is a prototype. It runs, but it has not yet been tested, and more work is needed before it can feed such a forecast with information that is complete, reliable and up to date. Turning what it extracts into a forecast, and bringing that forecast to the people who run the services, is the work of this project.

HEADSTART will do this in several stages. The first is to establish whether the tool can reliably extract the information needed from scientific papers. I will compare its results with information extracted manually from a set of studies, including papers that were not used during development. This will show how often the tool makes mistakes, which types of information are most difficult to extract, and whether some errors are more serious than others. I will then develop ways to detect these errors and account for them when using the extracted information in forecasts. The tool will also need to take in new studies as they appear without disturbing the information already collected.

The second stage is to turn this information into a forecast. Published studies report findings from different populations, places and periods, and their results do not always agree. I will develop methods to combine these findings while accounting for their reliability and their relevance to Geneva. The combined evidence will give the forecast its starting point when local data are still few. As calls and admissions from the current episode come in, the forecast will be updated to reflect them. The methods will also need to allow for changes over time, since what held in past seasons may no longer hold during a new outbreak or an unusually severe heat wave. The forecasts might also be expressed as levels of pressure rather than plain counts. A number of admissions means little on its own, but set against the beds a hospital has, or a number of calls against what a call centre can take on a normal day, it says how close the service is to its limit. Published studies and official plans already define such levels, and the capacity figures are public, so the two can be put together.

The third stage is to establish what HEADSTART should forecast and which data can support it. The project will assess the availability, quality and time resolution of the relevant health and environmental data, and define measurable outcomes that reflect the development of a health crisis. The initial work will focus on respiratory illness and the demand it creates for emergency and hospital care, because these epidemics put the heaviest and most regular pressure on the services and are followed by several data streams at once. Heat-related health problems come next, since they strain the same services and can be forecast from the same kind of data. Legionellosis, a rarer infection with an environmental source, is an exploratory extension. I am currently working on a predictive model of it for Geneva, and those findings will be a good fit to test the tool on a different kind of health threat. These applications involve different types of outcomes and different warning periods, so they will be studied separately rather than treated as if they were the same forecasting problem.

The fourth stage is to test whether the forecasts would have been useful in real time. I will reconstruct past forecasts using only the information that was available on each date. This is essential because a method can appear accurate when tested with information that would not actually have been available when a warning was needed. I will compare forecasts made with the published knowledge against the same forecasts made without it, so that the only difference is the knowledge itself, and against the simple methods the services rely on today. The evaluation will cover how early the forecast can detect a rise in demand, how often it misses an event, how often it issues a warning that is not followed by an increase, and how much uncertainty remains in its predictions. The tests will be repeated across different periods and health events to establish whether the results are consistent rather than driven by a few exceptional cases.

The fifth stage is to determine how well the approach works in Geneva and why it sometimes fails. Evidence from other places may improve predictions, but differences in populations, climate, healthcare services and the way cases are recorded may also make that evidence less useful. I will examine which types of evidence improve forecasts, which have little value, and which may lead to misleading predictions. This will help establish when published research can support local forecasting and when greater weight should be given to local data.

Finally, I will assess whether better predictions could make a practical difference. An accurate forecast is not necessarily useful if it arrives too late, gives little indication of the likely increase in demand, or cannot be translated into action. I will therefore ask the physicians, dispatchers and managers of these units what a missed event and an unnecessary alert cost them, use their answers to set the warning thresholds, and examine how warnings could be presented so that the services can prepare. If the earlier stages support it, this work could lead to a limited trial in which the forecast runs alongside normal practice, without directing operational decisions.

Together, these stages will establish whether published knowledge can be combined with current data to provide earlier and more reliable warnings of health crises. They will also show what is needed to make the approach work, where its limits lie, and whether its benefits justify the effort required to use it. The project will not assume that combining more information leads to better forecasts. That is one of the main claims HEADSTART is designed to test.
