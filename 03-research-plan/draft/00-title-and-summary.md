**HEADSTART: Forecasting health-system crises from published evidence when local outcome data are scarce**

# 1. Summary

At the start of a health crisis, many useful inputs are already there. Weather, air quality, calendar effects, wastewater and public surveillance data come from public feeds or established partners. What is missing is not this context. It is local, timely and reliable data on the outcome of interest for the episode that is starting. Past local records may be long, but they do not say how the new episode will affect that outcome. The model has to estimate this relationship early and update it as outcomes arrive.

HEADSTART asks whether published evidence from other settings and past events can improve forecasts before enough outcomes from the current episode are available, and whether the method can detect when that evidence is misleading. Published studies help identify plausible parameter values, relevant explanatory variables and their lags, suitable model forms, and outcome definitions and warning thresholds. A prior is a probability distribution for model parameters before it is updated with outcomes from the current episode. It represents uncertainty rather than treating a published estimate as exact. HEADSTART will extract this information, measure extraction error and assess how relevant each finding is to the local setting. The main focus is on comparing an evidence-informed strategy to a reference strategy. Both employ the same available context feeds, previous local outcomes, and the outcomes observed so far in the current episode. Only the evidence-informed strategy uses information extracted from published studies to refine its parameter distributions and model choices.

The work has four linked parts. First, I will compare automated extraction with a manual benchmark and measure how extraction errors change the uncertainty of the model inputs. Second, I will build a model that forecasts the chosen outcome and expresses it as ordered levels of pressure on the health system, with the number of levels and their thresholds taken first from the evidence and fixed before any test. Third, I will replay past events using only the information available at the time and compare the two strategies. Fourth, I will test whether differences in forecast quality would change operational decisions.

The tests take place in Geneva and cover three types of crisis with different causes. The main test uses respiratory epidemics, for which published evidence is comparatively rich. The second uses heat events. The third, in year four, uses legionellosis and a linked case-environment dataset to forecast case incidence. Before any clinical data are touched, the methods are validated on open Swiss series and released as a public benchmark. If published evidence improves forecasts, the project shows when and by how much. If it brings nothing or causes harm, the result says where it should not be used. For the crisis types that pass validation and that the services authorise, forecasts run on a daily dashboard in observation mode and are scored as outcomes arrive. The dashboard does not direct clinical care.

I trained as an econometrician and spent eleven years in quantitative finance on rare-event risk before a doctorate in biomedical sciences at Geneva. I conceived and built LiteRev with Aziza Merzouki and Olivia Keiser and extended it into LiteRev-Evidence, the prototype this project builds on. Through GESICA and GeoAI4EI (Horizon Europe) I work with Geneva's surveillance data and with European epidemic-intelligence partners. HEADSTART builds on this work to test a distinct question, in a programme I will lead.
